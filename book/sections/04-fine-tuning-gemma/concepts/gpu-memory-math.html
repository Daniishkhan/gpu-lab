Where Does All the GPU Memory Go?

P0 Core foundationabout 16 minutes6 quick checks · 1 CPU exercise

The #1 surprise for every software engineer running their first fine-tuning job is an instant CUDA out of memory crash on a model they just ran inference on five minutes earlier. Nothing is magical about GPU VRAM — once you learn five simple byte multipliers, you can budget any open model on the back of a napkin before renting a single cloud GPU.

By the end you'll be able to

  1. Estimate inference VRAM for any open model from its parameter count and precision (BF16, FP8, 4-bit).
  2. Explain why full fine-tuning needs 3× to 6× more memory than inference — and why LoRA and QLoRA slash that bill.
  3. Account for the two "invisible" memory hogs: the KV cache during serving and activations during training.
  4. Spot Gemma 4's three special memory quirks (PLE on E2B/E4B, 26B full-weight residency on MoE, and why NF4 hurts audio).

How to read this: top to bottom, answering every quick check before moving on. After finishing, test your intuition in the GPU Workbench and solve vram_budget.py locally with pytest. Builds on What Is a GPU and Why Do We Need One? and Inside Gemma 4's four architectures.

1Why did a 24 GB model blow up a 24 GB GPU?

You want to fine-tune Gemma 4 12B. You do quick mental math: 12 billion parameters × 2 bytes each ≈ 24 GB. You rent a 24 GB NVIDIA L4 GPU, load the model, call trainer.train(), and boom: OutOfMemoryError: Tried to allocate… on step 1.

Why? Because full fine-tuning Gemma 4 12B doesn't need 24 GB — it needs roughly 80 GB with an 8-bit optimizer, or 150+ GB with standard 32-bit AdamW! Yet switch one line to QLoRA and the exact same 12B model trains comfortably in ~11 GB on that same L4.

Same 12B weights, 7× difference in VRAM depending on what else has to sit on the GPU alongside them.

To never get blindsided by OOMs again, picture GPU VRAM as a carpenter's workbench (say 24 sq ft for an L4, or 80 sq ft for an H100). System RAM and SSD are a warehouse across the street. The GPU can only saw wood that sits right on the workbench — moving parts back and forth across the PCIe street slows everything down by 20×.

Let's walk through the five things competing for space on that workbench, one by one.

2Pile #1: The model weights themselves (precision math)

Every parameter in a neural network is just one number. How much workbench space do P billion parameters take? Multiply P by bytes per parameter:

PrecisionBytes / param10B-param mental mathWhere you use it
FP32 (32-bit float)4 bytes40 GBOptimizer master copies; almost never raw inference
BF16 / FP16 (16-bit)2 bytes20 GBStandard training & full-quality serving
FP8 / INT8 (8-bit)1 byte10 GBHigh-throughput serving (vLLM / SGLang)
NF4 / Q4_0 / W4A16 (4-bit)~0.5–0.55 bytes~5.5 GBQLoRA base weights & official Gemma 4 QAT serving

Add roughly 15–20% overhead for CUDA kernels, PyTorch allocator buffers, and quantization scale tables. That's why Gemma 4 12B in BF16 takes ~26.7 GB instead of a clean 24.0 GB — and why 4-bit QAT shrinks it to ~6.7 GB.

A dense 31B model is loaded for inference in 8-bit FP8 (1 byte per parameter, plus ~12–15% runtime overhead). Roughly how much GPU VRAM do the static weights need before adding context?

Right! 31B × 1 byte = 31 GB, plus ~12–15% overhead ≈ 35 GB (matching Gemma 4 31B's published 34.9 GB SFP8 footprint). ~70 GB is BF16 (2 bytes), and ~17.5 GB is 4-bit (0.5 bytes).

Use the mental-math rule: bytes = params × bytes_per_param. For 31B at 1 byte (8-bit), that starts at 31 GB before overhead — ~17 GB is 4-bit (0.5 bytes) and ~70 GB is 16-bit (2 bytes).

3Piles #2, #3 & #4: Why does training explode memory?

Inference only reads the furniture on the workbench. Training modifies every trainable screw. For every parameter you allow to update, PyTorch must keep three extra things on the workbench:

  1. Gradients (Pile #2): One number per trainable weight saying which way and how hard to nudge it. In BF16, that's +2 bytes per trainable parameter.
  2. Optimizer states (Pile #3): Standard AdamW doesn't just look at this step's gradient — it keeps two running averages (momentum + variance) per trainable weight in FP32, plus an FP32 master copy in mixed precision. That's +8 bytes (or up to 12 bytes with FP32 master weights) per trainable parameter! Switching to 8-bit AdamW compresses those two notebooks down to +2 bytes per trainable parameter.
  3. Activations (Pile #4): To compute gradients during the backward pass, PyTorch saves the intermediate outputs of every layer from the forward pass. Bigger batch sizes and longer sequences make this pile balloon fast — which is why gradient checkpointing throws away most activations and recomputes them during backprop, trading ~25% extra compute for massive VRAM savings.
Click each mode for a 12B model (seq len 2,048). Watch how freezing 99% of weights with LoRA collapses gradients and optimizer states to a sliver, and QLoRA shrinks the frozen base weights 4× further.
The whole secret of LoRA & QLoRA in one line: Gradients and optimizer states only exist for trainable parameters. Freeze 99% of the weights and train a 1% low-rank adapter, and Piles #2 and #3 drop by ~100×. Quantize the frozen base weights to 4-bit (QLoRA), and Pile #1 drops by ~4× too.

Why does switching from Full Fine-Tuning (8-bit AdamW) to BF16 LoRA save ~50 GB on Gemma 4 12B, even though the base weights are STILL stored in 16-bit BF16?

Nail on the head! Removing 2 bytes of gradients + 2–8 bytes of optimizer state across 12 billion frozen weights immediately deletes 48–120 GB of workbench clutter. (Converting the base weights to 4-bit is QLoRA, a separate step.)

Check the interactive bar above: in BF16 LoRA, the blue Weights bar doesn't shrink at all! What disappears are the orange Gradients and red Optimizer bars, because frozen weights never need gradients or AdamW momentum states.

4Pile #5: The KV cache (why serving breaks under concurrency)

During inference, there are no gradients or optimizer states. So why do production serving engines like vLLM still run out of memory? Because of the KV cache.

Remember from Chapter 1 that generation runs one token at a time. Without caching, generating token #2,000 would re-compute attention keys and values for all 1,999 earlier tokens from scratch. Instead, the GPU parks each token's Key and Value vectors on the workbench so every new step just reads them.

The catch: the KV cache grows linearly with both context length and the number of concurrent users:

KV cache bytes ≈ 2 (K and V)
               × num_layers
               × num_kv_heads × head_dim
               × bytes_per_element (2 in BF16)
               × sequence_length
               × concurrent_requests

One user with a 2,048-token prompt barely registers (~0.1–0.3 GB). Put 32 concurrent callers on a 16,384-token context and the KV cache suddenly consumes 30+ GB all by itself! That is why modern models use Grouped-Query Attention (fewer KV heads), sliding-window attention, and KV-sharing (all built into Gemma 4), and why vLLM uses PagedAttention to pack KV blocks without wasting space.

Your vLLM server serves 1 user at 4K context with 14 GB of VRAM used (12 GB weights + 2 GB KV cache). Traffic jumps to 8 concurrent users at 4K context. Roughly how much VRAM does it need now?

Exactly! Model weights are shared once across every concurrent request; only the KV cache multiplies by concurrency. That single insight explains why serving 8 users needs a 48 GB GPU even though the weights are only 12 GB.

Separate what is shared from what is per-request. Every user reads the exact same 12 GB weight matrices on the workbench — only each user's 2 GB KV cache needs its own separate slot (12 + 8×2 = 28 GB).

5Three Gemma 4 memory quirks that trip up real teams

Most online VRAM calculators get Gemma 4 wrong because they assume every model is a plain dense Transformer. Watch out for these three architectural realities:

  1. Why does "E2B" need 11.4 GB in BF16 instead of ~4 GB? The E stands for effective parameters per token. Gemma 4 E2B and E4B use Per-Layer Embeddings (PLE) — extra token lookup tables per layer (~5.1B total params on E2B, ~8.0B on E4B). Lookup tables cost almost zero compute (fast!), so the model runs like a 2B/4B model, but those static tables still sit on the workbench unless offloaded on mobile.
  2. Why does "26B-A4B" need 57.7 GB in BF16 when only 4B are active? Mixture-of-Experts activates only a few of its 128 experts per token (fast compute!), but every single expert must stay loaded in VRAM so the router can pick any expert on the next token.
  3. Why shouldn't you use 4-bit NF4 QLoRA for Gemma 4 audio fine-tuning? Uniform 4-bit NF4 quantizes audio-critical projection layers and visibly degrades speech recognition accuracy (WER). As Google's own audio guide notes, load E2B-it/E4B-it in BF16 LoRA with gradient checkpointing (~12–19 GB on one L4) or use selective quantization in Unsloth.
Go deeper: Gradient accumulation — shrinking activation memory without shrinking effective batch size

Suppose your recipe wants a global batch size of 32 examples per optimizer update, but batch_size=32 explodes activation memory and OOMs your 24 GB L4. Set per_device_train_batch_size=4 and gradient_accumulation_steps=8.

PyTorch runs 8 tiny forward+backward passes (keeping only 4 examples' activations on the workbench at a time), sums their gradients in place, and takes one mathematically identical optimizer step after the 8th micro-batch. Same math, 8× smaller activation peak.

Gemma 4 26B-A4B only activates 4B parameters per token. Can you load it in BF16 on a single 24 GB L4 GPU for inference?

Correct! MoE trades compute (FLOPs and decode speed scale with active 4B) for memory (VRAM scales with total 26B). That's why 4-bit QAT (~14.4 GB) is the sweet spot for serving 26B-A4B on a single 24 GB GPU.

The router decides at runtime, token by token which experts to wake up. If inactive experts lived on disk or CPU RAM, fetching them would stall every token by milliseconds. All 26B parameters must stay parked on the GPU workbench.

You want to fine-tune Gemma 4 E2B-it on custom accented speech recordings using a 24 GB L4 GPU. Should you pick 4-bit NF4 QLoRA or BF16 LoRA with gradient checkpointing?

Exactly! You already have 24 GB on an L4 — saving 5 GB with NF4 isn't worth hurting audio projection precision when BF16 LoRA fits with ~12 GB to spare.

On a 24 GB L4, E2B-it in BF16 LoRA with gradient checkpointing only takes ~12 GB. Uniform 4-bit NF4 quantizes audio-critical layers and hurts WER — save NF4 for text-only tasks or when VRAM is genuinely tight.

✓What to remember

  1. Static weights ≈ total_params × bytes_per_param (2 for BF16, 1 for FP8, ~0.55 for 4-bit), plus 15–20% CUDA/allocator overhead.
  2. Training adds gradients (2 B/trainable param), optimizer states (2 B in 8-bit AdamW, 8 B in FP32 AdamW), and activations (tamed with gradient checkpointing + gradient accumulation).
  3. LoRA freezes ~99% of weights (eliminating their gradients and optimizer states); QLoRA also compresses those frozen weights to 4-bit.
  4. During serving, weights are shared across users while KV cache multiplies by context_length × concurrent_users.
  5. On Gemma 4: remember total vs. effective params (PLE on E2B/E4B, all 26B resident on MoE 26B-A4B), and prefer BF16 LoRA for audio fine-tuning.

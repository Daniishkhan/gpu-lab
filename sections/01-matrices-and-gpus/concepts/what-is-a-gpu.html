What Is a GPU and Why Do We Need One?

P0 Core hardware intuitionabout 15 minutes6 quick checks · 1 CPU exercise

Everyone says "you need a GPU for AI," then treats the GPU like a magic fast box. Once you see what a GPU physically is — and the one brutal bottleneck that controls its speed — every mysterious ML fact suddenly clicks: why training needs huge batches, why generating text one word at a time barely uses a GPU's math power, why 4-bit quantization speeds up replies, and why a Mixture-of-Experts model like Gemma 4 26B-A4B talks as fast as a 4B model.

By the end you'll be able to

  1. Explain the physical difference between a CPU and a GPU using one kitchen picture.
  2. Calculate how many math operations one matrix multiply costs — and why it breaks a CPU.
  3. Separate a GPU's two completely different speed limits: cooking speed (compute) vs. hallway speed (memory bandwidth).
  4. Predict whether any AI workload (training, prompt prefill, or token-by-token decode) is limited by math or by moving bytes.

How to read this: top to bottom, answering every quick check before moving on. Builds on Matrix multiplication & tensor shapes and Everyday NumPy: slicing, axis, broadcasting & masks.

1Why does one single LLM layer crush a laptop CPU?

In the last chapter, you saw that one neural-network layer is just a matrix multiply: output = input @ weights.

Now plug in real numbers. Suppose a single layer has a 4,096 × 4,096 weight matrix, and you feed in a prompt of 4,096 tokens. Every output number needs 4,096 multiplies and 4,096 adds — and there are 4,096 × 4,096 output numbers. Total math operations: 2 × 4,096³ ≈ 137 billion operations — for one single layer (a real model has 30 to 60 layers!).

In plain Python loops, 137 billion operations takes hours. Even on an 8-core compiled C++ CPU, it takes a noticeable fraction of a second per layer. On one NVIDIA H100 GPU, those exact same 137 billion operations finish in 0.14 milliseconds.

How can one chip beat a modern CPU by 100× to 1,000× on the exact same math? Not because GPU cores are individually faster — each tiny GPU math unit is actually slower and dumber than a CPU core! The secret is how silicon area is spent.

A CPU minimizes time for one tricky task with lots of if/else branches. A GPU maximizes total throughput across millions of identical math ops at once.

Carry this single physical picture through the rest of the chapter: a CPU is 8 Michelin-star chefs who can improvise complex French recipes with lots of branching decisions, while a GPU is a stadium kitchen with 10,000 prep cooks who all make the exact same knife chop on the exact same beat. Give them 100,000 carrots to slice — identical independent work — and the 10,000 prep cooks obliterate the 8 master chefs.

Where the kitchen analogy breaks: human cooks bump elbows and need room; GPU cores execute identical instructions in synchronized squads (called warps of 32 threads) and modern AI GPUs even have special welded chopping blocks (Tensor Cores) that multiply whole small matrices in a single clock tick.

2Why does matrix multiplication love 10,000 prep cooks?

Look at any cell C[i, j] in a matrix product C = A @ B. It is the dot product of row i of A with column j of B. Notice what it does not depend on: it never waits for C[i, j-1] or C[i-1, j]!

Every output cell is 100% independent — zero waiting on neighbors:

C[0,0] = dot(A[0,:], B[:,0])  ← Cook #1
C[0,1] = dot(A[0,:], B[:,1])  ← Cook #2    all run on the
C[0,2] = dot(A[0,:], B[:,2])  ← Cook #3    EXACT SAME TICK
  ...
C[4095,4095]                  ← Cook #10,000+

And the same trick holds for almost every step in an LLM: multiplying inputs by weights, applying activations (ReLU/SwiGLU) to every number, computing attention scores across tokens, and nudging millions of weights during backpropagation. Originally built to shade millions of independent screen pixels for video games, GPUs turned out to be the perfect matrix-multiply engine.

Which of these two tasks will see a massive speedup on a GPU vs. a CPU?

Exactly! Pointer-chasing is strictly sequential — 9,999 prep cooks sit idle waiting for cook #1 to find the next pointer. Matrix multiplication has millions of independent dot products that run simultaneously.

Remember: individual GPU cores are actually slower than CPU cores on sequential branching or pointer chasing. GPUs only win when thousands of independent identical calculations can run at the same moment.

3The hidden twist: cooking speed vs. hallway speed

Now comes the single most important hardware insight in all of AI serving — the one that separates beginners from engineers who know why their server is slow.

A GPU has two completely separate speed limits:

  1. Cooking speed — Compute (FLOPs/sec): How many floating-point multiplies and adds the 10,000 cooks can perform per second (e.g., an NVIDIA L4 does 121 trillion BF16 ops/sec; an H100 does 989 trillion).
  2. Hallway speed — Memory Bandwidth (GB/sec): How fast ingredients (weights and vectors) can walk from the GPU's storage shelf (VRAM / HBM) into the cooks' hands (e.g., an L4 moves 300 GB/s; an H100 moves 3,350 GB/s).

Think about what happens when you ask those 10,000 cooks to process one single token (generating 1 word for 1 user) vs. a batch of 1,024 tokens (reading a prompt or training) through a 12B BF16 model (24 GB of weights):

  • Generating 1 token (batch = 1): You must walk all 24 GB of weights through the hallway from VRAM into the chip, do just 2 ops per weight (24 billion ops total), and throw the weights away! That's only 1 FLOP per byte fetched. On an H100 (which wants ~295 FLOPs per byte to stay busy!), the cooks finish chopping in 0.02 ms and then sit staring at the ceiling for 7.1 ms waiting for the hallway! You are memory-bandwidth-bound.
  • Processing 1,024 tokens at once (prefill or training): You walk those exact same 24 GB of weights through the hallway once, and reuse every weight across all 1,024 tokens! Now you do 1,024 FLOPs per byte fetched — the cooks stay 100% busy and you are compute-bound!
Toggle GPU and drag Tokens in Batch from 1 (single-user decode) up to 1,024 (prefill / batched serving). Watch how single-token decode wastes >99% of the GPU's compute waiting on the VRAM hallway — and why batching 16–64 users unlocks huge throughput for almost the same time per step!

That ratio — math ops divided by bytes moved — has a classic hardware name: Arithmetic Intensity (FLOPs/byte). The crossover point where cooking speed exactly equals hallway speed (peak_FLOPs / memory_bandwidth) is the GPU's ridge point (typically ~250–400 FLOPs/byte on modern NVIDIA GPUs). Below the ridge point, faster compute doesn't help — only moving fewer bytes or reusing weights across more tokens speeds you up!

The Roofline Law of LLMs: Single-user text generation (decode) is almost always memory-bandwidth-bound (limited by reading the weights out of VRAM once per token). Prompt reading (prefill) and training are compute-bound (limited by Tensor Core FLOPs).

You are serving Gemma 4 12B (BF16 = 24 GB) to 1 single user on an L4 GPU (300 GB/s VRAM bandwidth). Roughly what is the physical upper bound on decode speed (tokens/sec), ignoring overhead?

Spot on! That single back-of-the-envelope division (VRAM bandwidth / active weight GB) predicts real-world single-user LLM speed within ~20% across every GPU on earth! Drop those weights to 4-bit (~6.7 GB) and the exact same L4 jumps from ~12 tok/s to ~45 tok/s.

Remember: generating 1 token forces the GPU to read all 24 GB of weights across the 300 GB/s VRAM hallway once. Time per token ≥ 24 GB / 300 GB/s = 0.08 s → at most 1 / 0.08 = 12.5 tokens/sec, no matter how many TFLOPS the chip has!

On that same GPU during decode (memory-bandwidth-bound), what happens when you serve 8 concurrent users together in one batch instead of 1 user?

Yes!! That is the physical reason continuous batching in vLLM and SGLang exists: up until your batch hits the GPU's ridge point (hundreds of tokens!), those idle prep cooks can serve extra concurrent users for nearly free (as long as their KV caches fit in VRAM).

At batch size 1, over 99% of the GPU's math cores are sitting idle waiting for weights to walk down the VRAM hallway. Serving 8 users in one batch still walks the exact same 24 GB of weights down the hallway once — it just hands each weight to 8 idle cooks instead of 1!

4VRAM vs. System RAM: why PCIe is a tiny straw

A common beginner question: "My cloud machine has 128 GB of system RAM and only 24 GB of GPU VRAM — why can't I just keep a 60 GB model in system RAM and let the GPU read it?"

Compare the doorways:

Storage tierKitchen analogyTypical sizeBandwidth (doorway width)
GPU SRAM (L1 / shared mem)Chef's cutting boardtens of MB~15,000 – 30,000 GB/s
GPU VRAM (GDDR6 / HBM3)Kitchen counter right next to cooks24 – 80 GB300 – 3,350 GB/s
CPU System RAM (via PCIe)Warehouse across the street64 – 512 GB32 – 64 GB/s (10–50× narrower!)
NVMe SSDSupplier across town1 – 4 TB3 – 7 GB/s

If a 60 GB model spills off the GPU counter into CPU RAM, every single generated token has to drag gigabytes across the tiny 32 GB/s PCIe straw — crushing decode speed from 40 tok/s down to ~0.5 tok/s. That is why every serving and fine-tuning job starts with one question: does it fit in VRAM?

You enable CPU offloading so a 50 GB model runs on a 24 GB GPU. Why does generation suddenly crawl at ~1 token per second?

Exactly — PCIe is 10× to 50× narrower than GPU VRAM bandwidth. Offloading saves you from crashing on a quick test, but is almost never viable for real-time serving or phone calls.

The math still happens on the chip, but the ingredients have to walk across a narrow street (PCIe at ~32–64 GB/s) on every single token instead of sitting on the kitchen counter (VRAM at 300–3,350 GB/s).

5Four AI mysteries this one model immediately unlocks

Everything you'll build in Stages 2–7 now makes physical sense from one single diagram:

  1. Why does 4-bit quantization (QAT) make Gemma 4 reply ~3–4× faster? Not mainly because 4-bit math is faster — because 4-bit weights are 4× fewer bytes to haul down the VRAM hallway on every decode token!
  2. Why does Gemma 4 26B-A4B (MoE) generate tokens as fast as a 4B model? Even though all 26B weights sit in VRAM, each token only reads the 4B active weights across the hallway!
  3. Why does FlashAttention speed up long prompts? It fuses attention steps inside the ultra-fast tiny SRAM cutting board (~20,000 GB/s) so huge intermediate attention matrices never take round-trips to VRAM.
  4. Why does our voice agent care so much about LLM TTFT vs. decode tok/s? Prefill (reading the conversation so far) hits compute speed, while decode (emitting tokens to TTS) hits VRAM bandwidth — and both must stay tiny to beat the 800 ms caller threshold.

Gemma 4 26B-A4B (Mixture-of-Experts) holds 26B parameters in VRAM, while Gemma 4 31B is dense. Why does 26B-A4B decode tokens roughly 5–6× faster for a single caller on the same GPU?

Nail on the head! You still need enough VRAM to hold all 26B weights on the counter, but per token the hallway only carries the 4B weights the router chose — ~6× fewer bytes moved per token.

Tie it back to the hallway rule (tok/s ≈ VRAM_bandwidth / bytes_read_per_token). A dense 31B model drags all 31B weights through the hallway for every single word; 26B-A4B only drags the 4B active weights for that token.

If you double a GPU's raw compute (TFLOPS) while keeping VRAM bandwidth identical, which workload speeds up almost 2× and which barely moves?

Exactly! Upgrading compute speeds up compute-bound work (training & prefill), while speeding up single-user decode requires higher VRAM bandwidth, smaller/quantized weights, or speculative decoding (which checks multiple draft tokens in one weight-read!).

Look back at the interactive roofline widget: single-user decode is starved by the VRAM hallway, not by cook speed. Giving idle cooks faster knives won't deliver ingredients faster — only compute-bound large batches (training / prefill) use those extra TFLOPS.

✓What to remember

  1. A GPU trades a few clever serial CPU cores for thousands of simple parallel cores + Tensor Cores — ideal for independent matrix math, terrible for pointer chasing.
  2. A single matrix multiply (M, K) @ (K, N) performs 2 × M × K × N FLOPs.
  3. Every GPU has two speed walls: compute (FLOPs/s) and VRAM bandwidth (GB/s). Their ratio (FLOPs/s ÷ GB/s) is the ridge point.
  4. Below the ridge point (single-user decode) you are memory-bandwidth-bound (max tok/s ≈ VRAM_BW / active_weight_GB); above it (training, long prefill, big batches) you are compute-bound.
  5. Keep active weights in VRAM — spilling across PCIe to CPU RAM narrows your doorway by 10–50×.
